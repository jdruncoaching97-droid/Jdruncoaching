Shin pain in runners (shin splints)

What is behind pain on the inner edge of the shin, how to keep training while it settles, and when it needs a closer look.

By Joe, musculoskeletal physiotherapist and Level 1 Australian Athletics Run Coach  |  Updated October 2026

The short version

  • Shin pain is usually the bone and muscle being loaded faster than they can adapt, so the fix is managing load, not complete rest.
  • Cut hills, speed and jumping first, reduce volume for a week or two, and use 3/10 pain as your guide.
  • Build calf strength, then return step by step, changing one thing at a time.
  • Pinpoint bone tenderness, night pain, or pain that is not improving needs assessment.

What it is

"Shin splints" is the everyday name for pain along the inner edge of the shin bone (tibia). The clinical term is usually medial tibial stress syndrome. It happens when the bone and the muscles attached to it are loaded faster than they can adapt, so it is a load problem, not a mystery injury.

It sits on a spectrum. At one end is an irritable, sore shin. At the other is a stress fracture. That is why the details of your pain matter.

Who gets it

  • Runners who have jumped in volume, speed or hills quickly, often after time off or a new training block.
  • Runners who have added jumping or plyometrics on top of their running.
  • Anyone changing shoes, surfaces or running more on hard, cambered roads.
  • Runners who are under-fuelling or who have low energy availability, which weakens bone's ability to adapt.
  • Runners with limited calf strength or endurance, which leaves the shin to absorb more of each step.

What it usually feels like

  • A dull ache along a stretch of the inner shin, usually the lower half to two thirds, not a single spot.
  • Sore at the start of a run, eases as you warm up, and returns afterwards or the next morning.
  • Tender when you press along the inner edge of the bone.

What to change this week

You usually don't need to stop running completely. You need to reduce the load that provokes it, then rebuild.

  • Use pain as a guide. A reasonable rule is pain of 3/10 or less while running, settling within a few hours, and no worse the next morning. If it climbs above that, drop back a step.
  • Cut the spikes first. Pause hill repeats, fast reps, and jumping. Keep runs easy and on flat, even ground.
  • Reduce total load. Many people settle by running 30-50% less for a week or two, spread over shorter, more frequent runs, and keeping some days off.
  • Keep your fitness. Cycling, pool running or the cross trainer maintain your aerobic base while the shin settles.
  • Check the basics: shoes that are worn out or very different from what you normally wear, and any recent step up in surface or hills.

Build the calf

Strong, enduring calves share the job of absorbing each landing. Two to three times a week:

  • Straight-knee calf raises and bent-knee calf raises, slow (about 3 seconds up and 3 down), 3 sets of 8-12, adding weight as it gets easy.
  • Hip strength such as single-leg bridges and step-ups, so the whole leg shares the work.
  • Stay within comfortable pain levels. A little shin ache while lifting is fine; sharp pain is not.

Getting back to normal running

Change one thing at a time: first how often you run, then how long, then how fast. A sensible order:

StepWhat to doMove on when
1Walk 30-45 minutesNo shin pain during or the next morning
2Run-walk, such as 2 min run and 1 min walk, 6-8 times, every second dayPain 3/10 or less and settles by next morning, for 2-3 sessions
3Lengthen the running, shorten the walkingSame rule
4Continuous easy running, 20-30 minutes, every second daySame rule
5Add a run day, then total timeSame rule
6Bring back strides, then hills, then faster work, lastNormal training again

If a step flares the shin, go back one step for a few days. This usually takes weeks, not days.

Common mistakes

  • Resting completely for a long time, then returning at full load.
  • Stretching the shin or massaging hard on the bone as the main treatment.
  • Running through rising pain because "it eases once I warm up".
  • Changing shoes, surface and training all at once.

Get it assessed if

Shin pain that behaves differently needs a proper look, because it can be a bone stress injury.

  • The pain is in one small spot you can cover with a fingertip.
  • It hurts when walking, at rest, or wakes you at night.
  • Hopping on the leg reproduces the pain.
  • You have swelling, or it has not improved after 2-3 weeks of reduced load.
  • You have also had irregular or missing periods, repeated bone injuries, or you are struggling to eat enough for your training. These point to low energy availability, which needs its own plan.
