Fuelling for 5K to half marathon

What to eat every day, before, during and after training and racing, in practical amounts.

By Joe, musculoskeletal physiotherapist and Level 1 Australian Athletics Run Coach  |  Updated October 2026

The short version

  • Carbohydrate is the main fuel. Match the amount to the day: more on long and hard days, less on easy days.
  • For 5K and 10K, eat normally with a good meal 2-3 hours before. A half marathon benefits from some fuelling during the race.
  • Practise race-day fuelling in training. Never try something new on race day.
  • Repeated bone injuries, missed periods or constant fatigue are signs to see a sports dietitian.

The basics, every day

Training is the stress. Food is how you adapt to it. Most runners training for a 5K to a half marathon do well with ordinary food, eaten regularly and in enough quantity.

  • Carbohydrate is the main fuel for running. As a guide, about 3-5 g per kg of body weight a day on lighter training days, 5-7 g/kg on a typical training day, and 6-10 g/kg on heavy or long-run days.
  • Protein supports recovery and strength training: about 1.2-2.0 g/kg a day, spread across 3-5 meals, with roughly 20-40 g each time.
  • Fat and colour: the rest of your energy comes from fats, fruit and vegetables, and dairy or alternatives. Do not cut a food group without a reason.
  • Eat enough overall. Low energy availability (not eating enough for the training you do) is the core problem behind Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs), described in the 2023 IOC consensus. It affects runners of any sex and shows up as bone stress injuries, frequent illness, poor recovery and mood changes, and in women missed or irregular periods. Chronic intakes below roughly 30 kcal per kg of fat-free mass a day are linked to impaired body function.

Before a key session or race

  • Eat a carbohydrate-rich meal 2-3 hours before: toast or oats, banana, rice, pasta, with little fat and fibre.
  • A small top-up 30-60 minutes before if you are hungry: a banana, a gel, or a slice of toast with jam.
  • For 5K and 10K you do not need to "carb load". Eat normally with a bit more carbohydrate the day before.
  • For a half marathon, a modest increase in carbohydrate over the last day or two can help.
  • Do not try anything new on race day.

During running

  • Under 45 minutes: carbohydrate is not needed. Water is enough.
  • 45-75 minutes at a hard effort: small amounts help, and even rinsing your mouth with a carbohydrate drink can lift performance.
  • 1 to 2.5 hours: aim for about 30-60 g of carbohydrate per hour. A gel is typically 20-25 g, and a sports drink has about 6 g per 100 ml.
  • Over 2.5 hours: up to about 90 g per hour, using a glucose and fructose blend (most sports gels and drinks are), because the gut absorbs more of it. Build up to this gradually.
  • More is not always better. Recent studies of 120 g per hour found lower absorption efficiency and no clear performance gain over 90 g, in cyclists, who tolerate it better than runners. For most runners 90 g per hour is a ceiling, not a target.
  • Practise your race-day fuelling on long runs. Gut training can reduce stomach discomfort at higher intakes, so build up over weeks.
  • Drink to thirst, and try to avoid losing more than about 2% of body weight in sweat on long, hot runs. Drinking far more than you need can be as harmful as drinking too little. In hot conditions, include some sodium.

Afterwards

  • Eat within an hour or two, with carbohydrate and protein. Roughly 1 g/kg of carbohydrate and 0.25-0.3 g/kg of protein (about 20-25 g) is a good target.
  • If you have another run within 8 hours, recovery food matters more: aim for about 1-1.2 g/kg of carbohydrate per hour for the first 4 hours. Otherwise, your next normal meal is enough.
  • Fluids with a little salt help if you were sweating a lot.

Easy options that work

  • Pre-run: toast with honey and a banana, or a bowl of oats.
  • On the run: a gel or chews with water, or a diluted sports drink.
  • Post-run: yoghurt with fruit and muesli, eggs on toast, a chicken and rice bowl, or a milk-based smoothie.

Caffeine, iron and supplements

  • Caffeine at about 3-6 mg/kg an hour before can improve performance for some runners, but test it in training first.
  • If you feel flat for weeks, get a blood test. Iron and vitamin D are the usual suspects, and are best treated on the basis of results.
  • Supplements are optional. Food comes first.

Get individual advice if

Individual advice from an accredited sports dietitian is worth it if:

  • You have repeated bone or stress injuries, or irregular or missing periods.
  • You are constantly tired, getting sick often, or struggling to hit your training targets.
  • You are cutting calories while training hard, or finding food and weight take up a lot of your thinking.
  • You get stomach problems that do not settle with practice.
  • You have a medical condition such as diabetes or coeliac disease.
