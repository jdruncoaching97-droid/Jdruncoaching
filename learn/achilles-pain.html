Achilles pain in runners

Why the tendon above your heel gets sore, why complete rest is rarely the answer, and how to rebuild with progressive calf loading.

By Joe, musculoskeletal physiotherapist and Level 1 Australian Athletics Run Coach  |  Updated October 2026

The short version

  • Achilles pain is a tendon coping poorly with load, and it usually improves with the right load, not with rest alone.
  • Remove hills, speed and jumping for now, keep easy running if pain stays at 2-3/10 or less, settles within 24 hours and there is no night pain.
  • Slow, heavy calf raises 3 times a week are the core treatment; isometric holds can settle pain in the short term.
  • A sudden pop, loss of push-off, or pain after antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin needs prompt assessment.

What it is

Achilles tendinopathy is a tendon that is not coping with the load placed on it. It is not simple inflammation, and "resting until it stops hurting" rarely fixes it. Tendons respond to the right amount of load, so the aim is to keep loading them in a controlled way.

There are two common patterns. Midportion pain sits in the tendon, about 2-6 cm above the heel bone. Insertional pain sits right at the back of the heel where the tendon attaches. They are treated slightly differently, so it helps to know which you have.

What it usually feels like

  • Stiffness and soreness first thing in the morning, or after sitting, that eases within minutes of moving.
  • Pain at the start of a run that settles as you warm up, then can come back afterwards.
  • A thickened or tender area in the tendon, especially midportion.
  • Worse with hills, speed work, and any jumping or bounding.

What to change this week

  • Don't stop completely. Complete rest is not recommended. Keep running while you meet the pain rule: pain of 2-3/10 or less while running, settling within 24 hours, with no night pain, and morning stiffness is no worse the next day.
  • Remove the spikes. Hills, intervals and plyometrics load the tendon hardest, so swap them for easy flat running for now.
  • Keep your weekly total steady, or trim it a little. Avoid big jumps in one run.
  • Check what changed: a new block, a new shoe, a lower heel drop, a new surface.
  • A small heel lift in your shoes can reduce tendon strain for a short period while you rebuild.

Load the tendon, don't baby it

Progressive calf strengthening is the backbone of treatment.

  • Isometric holds to settle pain: standing calf raise held on both feet or one, 5 x 30-45 seconds, with 2 minutes between holds. This often eases pain for a few hours and helps you get moving.
  • Dose: the 2024 JOSPT guideline gives tendon loading its strongest (grade A) recommendation, at least 3 times a week and as heavy as symptoms allow. Improvement can start within about 2 weeks and often continues for 12 weeks or more.
  • Slow heavy calf raises, 3 times a week: straight-knee and bent-knee, 3 seconds up and 3 seconds down, 3-4 sets of 6-15 repetitions. Start with body weight, then add load (a backpack, dumbbells) as it becomes easy.
  • Later, add speed and spring: skipping, hopping and bounding return last, once heavy loading is comfortable.
  • For insertional pain, avoid letting the heel drop below the step during calf raises. Keep the movement on a flat floor.

Getting back to normal running

Once morning stiffness and day-to-day pain have settled, rebuild in this order:

StepWhat to doMove on when
1Keep the calf programme going; walk and cycleDaily pain at 2/10 or less and morning stiffness settling
2Easy flat running every second day, 20-30 minutesPain 2-3/10 or less, settled within 24 hours, no night pain, for 2-3 sessions
3Add run days, then total timeSame rule
4Add gentle hills, then strides, then faster workNo flare the next day

Expect this to take a number of weeks, and often a few months for stubborn cases. Keep the calf work going even after the pain has gone.

Common mistakes

  • Resting completely, then returning at full volume. The tendon is no better prepared.
  • Heavy stretching of the calf, which can aggravate insertional pain.
  • Going straight from pain-free to hills and speed in the same week.
  • Stopping the strength work as soon as it feels better.

Get it assessed if

Most Achilles pain is tendinopathy, but a few things need a faster look.

  • A sudden sharp pain or "pop" at the back of the leg, a gap in the tendon, or you cannot push off or rise onto your toes. This can be a tear, so get seen the same day.
  • The area is red, hot and swollen, or you have pain at rest and at night.
  • You are taking, or recently took, a fluoroquinolone antibiotic (for example ciprofloxacin) and have new tendon pain. Speak to your doctor promptly.
  • Pain is not improving after 6-8 weeks of sensible loading, or you are not sure whether it is midportion or insertional.
