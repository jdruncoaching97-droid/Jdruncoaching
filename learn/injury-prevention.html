Injury prevention for runners

Six habits that keep you running, and a simple traffic-light system for niggles.

By Joe, musculoskeletal physiotherapist and Level 1 Australian Athletics Run Coach  |  Updated October 2026

The short version

  • Most running injuries are load problems: too much, too soon, or too much going on at once.
  • Build gradually, change one thing at a time, include lighter weeks, and keep most running easy.
  • Strength training, sleep and enough food are as important as the running.
  • Score niggles from 0 to 10: green carry on, amber trim the load, red stop and get checked.

Most running injuries are load problems

Most running injuries come from doing more than your body is currently ready for, whether that is too fast a build, a sudden change, or a full life on top of training. The good news is that these are largely within your control.

1. Build gradually and stay consistent

  • Increase weekly volume in modest steps, and avoid doubling any single run's length overnight. The popular "10% rule" is not well supported: a systematic review found very limited evidence linking load changes to injury, and no clear difference between 10% and larger weekly increases. What matters is how your body responds, so judge progress by the pain rule and recovery.
  • Include a lighter week about every third or fourth week.
  • Change one thing at a time: distance, speed, hills, shoes or surface.
  • Keep most of your running easy. Hard sessions are the exception.
  • Be careful returning from a break. Fitness comes back faster than your bones and tendons.

2. Lift weights

  • Strength training two to three times a week improves running economy and is linked with fewer injuries.
  • Focus on squat and hinge patterns, single-leg work, calves and the hips, lifting heavy enough that the last reps are hard.
  • Add jumping and plyometric work gradually and fresh, with a cap on contacts.

3. Sleep and fuel

  • Aim for 7-9 hours of sleep. Athletes who regularly sleep less than 8 hours have higher injury rates.
  • Eat enough to match training. Low energy availability weakens bone and slows recovery.
  • Life stress counts. A stressful week at work is a good week to run less.

4. Recover and rotate

  • Rotate two pairs of shoes, and replace them before they are completely worn out.
  • Vary surfaces where you can, and avoid running on heavy camber.
  • A warm-up with easy running and a few strides before faster sessions is worthwhile.

5. Use a traffic light for niggles

Almost every injury starts as a niggle. The skill is acting early. Score the pain from 0 to 10:

LightWhat it looks likeWhat to do
Green2-3/10 or less during the run, settled within 24 hours, no night painCarry on. Keep noting it.
AmberAbove 3/10, or still sore after 24 hours, or a niggle that has lasted several daysCut hills and speed, shorten runs until the pain rule is met again, add strength work for that area.
RedNight pain, pain at rest, a limp, a change in how you run, pinpoint bone pain, or pain climbing despite backing offStop running, keep moving gently, and get it assessed.

Coaching athletes log their niggle score on every run and in the weekly check-in, so amber turns up before it becomes red.

6. Keep a log

  • Note distance, effort, sleep and any niggles. Patterns show up in a log long before they feel obvious.
  • Look back after any injury. The cause is usually in the previous 2-4 weeks.

Stop and get checked if

Stop and get checked, rather than trying to run through it, if you have:

  • Pain at a single bony point, or pain that hurts when you walk or hop.
  • Pain that wakes you or is there at rest.
  • A sudden pop, swelling or bruising.
  • A niggle that keeps coming back in the same place after you have rested it.
