Hamstring and calf strains in runners

What to do in the first days after a muscle strain, how to rebuild strength, and why speed comes back last.

By Joe, musculoskeletal physiotherapist and Level 1 Australian Athletics Run Coach  |  Updated October 2026

The short version

  • Early gentle movement beats long rest. Avoid heavy stretching and anything sharp in the first week.
  • Progress from pain-free walking to strength work, then jogging, then strides, with speed work last.
  • Nordic curls for hamstrings and slow heavy calf raises reduce the chance of the same injury coming back.
  • A pop, big bruising, a visible dip, or calf swelling and warmth without injury need prompt care.

What it is

A muscle strain is a partial tear of muscle fibres. In runners the hamstring (back of the thigh) and the calf are the usual victims. Hamstring strains typically happen during fast running or stretching. Calf strains typically happen when pushing off or accelerating, and are most often in the inner side of the upper calf.

What it usually feels like

  • A sudden sharp or pulling pain during a run, often making you slow or stop.
  • Tenderness at one spot in the muscle, sometimes bruising a day or two later.
  • Pain stretching the muscle and contracting against resistance.
  • Mild strains are tight and sore. Moderate strains hurt walking. Severe strains can cause a lot of bruising and a visible dip in the muscle.

The first few days

  • Protect, then move. Stop running, and walk as normally as pain allows. Early gentle movement is better than long rest.
  • Avoid heavy stretching in the first week, and avoid anything that provokes sharp pain.
  • Cycling or pool work is fine if it doesn't reproduce the pain.
  • A heel raise in your shoes can ease a calf strain when walking.

Rebuilding the muscle

Progress by criteria, not the calendar: pain settling, strength returning, and each step tolerated without a flare. The 2023 London consensus on hamstring injury agreed there are no fixed timelines. Walking and early exercise should stay within the pain rule, but sprinting should be pain free before you return to full speed. The first month back carries the highest risk of re-injury, so build speed gradually.

Hamstring

  • Early: isometric bridge holds with the heel on the floor, 5 x 30 seconds, then single-leg bridges.
  • Next: Romanian deadlifts, hamstring curls, bridge walkouts, 3 sets of 8-12 with load added slowly.
  • Later: Hip-dominant work and Nordic hamstring curls are commonly used to build strength at long muscle lengths once you are strong. Evidence that Nordics alone prevent injury is mixed, so treat them as part of a wider programme.

Calf

  • Early: pain-free walking, then seated and standing calf raises on both legs.
  • Next: single-leg straight-knee and bent-knee raises, 3 sets of 8-12, slowly and with load.
  • Later: hopping and skipping, before returning to running.

Getting back to running

StepWhat to doMove on when
1Pain-free walking and pain-free strength exercises, including single-leg versionsNo pain, and strength is close to the other side
2Easy jog, run-walk on flat groundPain 2/10 or less and no worse next day, for 2-3 sessions
3Continuous easy running, then add distanceSame rule
4Strides at 70%, then 80%, then 90% of top speed, over a number of sessionsFully comfortable, without pulling or tightness
5Hills and speed work, lastNormal training again

The commonest cause of re-injury is returning to fast running too soon, so speed is the last thing to come back.

Common mistakes

  • Stretching hard to "loosen" a fresh tear.
  • Returning to fast running as soon as walking is pain-free.
  • Skipping the strength work and relying on a gentle jog.
  • Ignoring the same twinge repeatedly instead of addressing the cause.

Get it assessed if

Some muscle injuries need to be seen early.

  • You felt or heard a pop, have a lot of bruising, a visible dip in the muscle, or cannot walk without a limp.
  • Pain at the sit bone at the top of the hamstring after a sudden sprint or stretch.
  • Numbness, tingling or weakness in the leg or foot.
  • Calf pain with swelling, warmth or redness that came on without a clear injury, or after long travel or inactivity. This can be a blood clot, so seek medical care urgently.
  • No steady improvement over 1-2 weeks.
