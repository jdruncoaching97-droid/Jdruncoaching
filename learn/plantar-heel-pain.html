Plantar heel pain in runners

Pain under the heel and arch, especially the first steps of the morning, and why loading beats long rests.

By Joe, musculoskeletal physiotherapist and Level 1 Australian Athletics Run Coach  |  Updated October 2026

The short version

  • Plantar heel pain is the fascia under the foot loaded harder than it is prepared for. It usually improves with the right load, not with a long rest.
  • Cut hills, speed and hard surfaces first, use the 2-3/10 rule as your guide, and support the foot in the short term.
  • Slow, heavy calf raises with the toes lifted, built up over weeks, are the most reliable fix.
  • Numbness, constant pain, night pain, or no improvement after 6-8 weeks needs assessment.

What it is

Plantar heel pain, often called plantar fasciitis or plantar fasciopathy, is pain under the heel and along the inside of the arch. The plantar fascia is a thick band of tissue that supports the arch and takes a large share of load with every step. When it is loaded harder than it is prepared for, it becomes sore and sensitive. Like other tendon-type problems, it responds to the right load, not to long rests.

Who gets it

  • Runners who have stepped up volume, speed or hills quickly.
  • People on their feet for long hours, or who have recently changed footwear or gone barefoot more.
  • Runners with limited calf strength or ankle movement.
  • Returning runners after a break, especially a rushed return.

What it usually feels like

  • Sharp or deep pain under the heel on your first steps out of bed or after sitting, easing within a few minutes.
  • Pain that warms up during a run, then returns afterwards or later in the day.
  • Tenderness when pressing on the inside front edge of the heel.
  • Worse on hard floors, barefoot, or in flat, thin-soled shoes.

What to change this week

  • Use pain as a guide. Our rule: pain of 2-3/10 or less while running, settling within 24 hours, with no night pain. First steps next morning should be no worse.
  • Remove the heaviest loads first: hill running, speed work, hard surfaces and jumping. Keep runs shorter and easy.
  • Support the foot for now. A cushioned shoe with some arch support, and avoiding barefoot walking on hard floors, can ease pain while you rebuild. Taping or an off-the-shelf insole can help in the short term.
  • Keep your fitness with cycling or pool running on days the foot is sore.

Load the fascia

Current guidelines support a combination: calf and plantar fascia stretching, taping in the short term, and progressive strengthening. Heavy, slow loading is the part that builds capacity, so do not skip it.

  • Calf raise with the toes lifted: stand with a rolled towel under your toes so the big toe is pulled upwards, then rise onto the ball of the foot slowly (about 3 seconds up, a 2 second hold, 3 seconds down). Start with 3 sets of 12, every second day.
  • Make it harder over weeks: add a backpack with weight, and aim for 3 sets of 8-10 at a load that feels hard by the last reps.
  • Straight-knee and bent-knee calf raises on the floor, 3 sets of 8-12.
  • Some soreness while you do these is normal. Sharp or worsening pain is not.

Gentle calf and plantar fascia stretching can help morning stiffness, but treat it as a supporting act. Corticosteroid injections can give short-term relief but come with risks to the fascia and heel pad, so discuss them with a clinician before you consider one.

Getting back to normal running

This one is slow. Expect improvement over 6-12 weeks, and sometimes longer if it has been there for months.

Keep the loaded calf work going two to three times a week even when you feel better.

Common mistakes

  • Resting for weeks, then going straight back to full running.
  • Relying on rolling a ball under the foot as the only treatment.
  • Returning to hills and speed in the same week as returning to running.
  • Quitting the calf strengthening as soon as the morning pain eases.

Get it assessed if

Most heel pain is plantar fasciopathy, but some patterns need a proper look.

  • The pain began with a sudden pop or a fall, or you cannot put weight through the heel.
  • Squeezing the heel from both sides is painful (this can point to a heel bone stress injury).
  • You have numbness, tingling or burning in the heel or foot, or pain that is constant or wakes you at night.
  • The heel is red, hot or swollen, or you have pain in both heels along with back stiffness or other sore joints.
  • It has not improved after about 6-8 weeks of sensible loading.
