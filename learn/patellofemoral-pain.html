Patellofemoral knee pain in runners

An ache at the front of the knee around the kneecap, how to keep training, and the strength work that helps.

By Joe, musculoskeletal physiotherapist and Level 1 Australian Athletics Run Coach  |  Updated October 2026

The short version

  • Front-of-knee pain is usually the kneecap joint loaded faster than it can adapt, and is not a sign of damaged or worn knees.
  • Reduce downhill running, stairs and deep squats for now, keep runs short and flat, and keep pain at 2-3/10 or less, settling within 24 hours.
  • Consistent quad and hip strengthening for 6-8 weeks is the main long-term treatment.
  • Locking, giving way, swelling after a twist, or night pain needs assessment.

What it is

Patellofemoral pain is pain at the front of the knee, around or behind the kneecap. It is one of the most common running knee problems. The kneecap sits in a groove at the end of the thigh bone, and the joint becomes irritated when its load goes up faster than it can adapt. It is a load-tolerance problem, not a sign of "bad" or "worn out" knees.

Who gets it

  • Runners who have increased weekly distance, hills or downhill running quickly.
  • Runners returning after a break, or starting a new block.
  • Runners with less quadriceps and hip strength than their running volume needs.
  • People who sit for long periods, then run.

What it usually feels like

  • A diffuse ache at the front of the knee, often hard to pin to one spot.
  • Worse going downstairs, squatting, running downhill, and after sitting with bent knees for a long time.
  • It may warm up during a run, then ache afterwards.
  • Sometimes a grinding feeling with no sharp pain. That alone is not a problem.

What to change this week

  • Reduce what provokes it: downhill running, stairs and deep squats. Keep running on flat ground at an easy pace for now.
  • Use pain as a guide. Our rule: pain of 2-3/10 or less while running, settling within 24 hours, with no night pain.
  • Shorten, don't stop. Shorter, more frequent runs usually settle better than one long run.
  • Raise your step rate. Start with a 5-10% increase on easy runs (use a metronome or watch), which lowers load through the knee and hip. Once that feels natural, build gradually toward about 180 steps a minute. 180 is a coaching target, not a magic number: the research mostly tests the 5-10% change, and the best rate depends on your height and pace.
  • Keep your fitness with cycling (keep the saddle high enough to avoid deep knee bend) or pool running.
  • Taping the kneecap or a change of shoe can help some people in the short term.

Strengthen the quads and hips

Strengthening the thigh and hip, done consistently, is the main long-term treatment. Aim for two to three sessions a week for at least 6-8 weeks.

  • For pain relief at first: wall sit or leg extension isometric holds, 5 x 30-45 seconds, at an effort that is comfortable.
  • Quads: leg press, split squats and step-ups, 3 sets of 8-12, adding load slowly. Work in a range that is comfortable.
  • Hips: side-lying leg raises or banded side steps, single-leg bridges, and hip thrusts, 3 sets of 10-15.
  • Calves: straight-knee and bent-knee raises, 3 sets of 8-12.

Getting back to normal running

1Walking and cycling comfortably, strength programme startedPain 2/10 or less in daily life, stairs are easier
2Run-walk on flat ground, every second dayPain 2-3/10 or less, settled within 24 hours, no night pain, for 2-3 sessions
3Lengthen the running, shorten the walking, then add a run daySame rule
4Add gentle rolling hills, then descents, then faster work, lastNormal training again

Common mistakes

  • Stopping all exercise and loading, so the knee gets weaker.
  • Squatting very deep or loading heavily through pain.
  • Relying on foam rolling and stretching without strengthening.
  • Returning to downhill running too early.

Get it assessed if

Most front-of-knee pain is patellofemoral pain, but some signs point somewhere else.

  • The knee locks, catches so you cannot straighten it, or gives way.
  • You had a fall or a twist with immediate swelling.
  • The knee is red, hot and swollen, or you have a fever.
  • Pain at night or at rest that does not settle.
  • No improvement after 6-8 weeks of consistent strengthening and adjusted running.
